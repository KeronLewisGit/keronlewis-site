You can land remote developer jobs from the Caribbean, but you have to apply differently from someone in Toronto or Austin. Filter for roles that hire "worldwide" or "Americas", lead with your time-zone overlap, show shipped work rather than certificates, and sort out how you'll get paid in US dollars before the offer arrives.

Most of the work is in that last stretch. Plenty of Caribbean developers have the skills. Fewer get through screening, because their applications go to jobs they can't legally take, their CVs don't parse, or they can't answer "how do we pay you?" with confidence.

I'm a full-stack developer in Port of Spain. I've been building for clients since 2016, I'm looking for remote work myself right now, and I've worked with businesses across the region and in North America. This is what I've learned about the process, written for developers in Trinidad and Tobago, Jamaica, Barbados, Guyana and the rest of the region.

## Why Caribbean developers have a real edge for remote roles

Start with the advantages, because you should be putting them in every application.

- **Time zone.** Trinidad and Tobago, Barbados and Guyana sit on Atlantic Standard Time (UTC-4) all year with no daylight saving. That puts us on the same clock as New York from March to early November, and one hour ahead for the rest of the year. Jamaica is on UTC-5 year round. A US team gets almost a full shared working day with you, which is something a developer in Manila or Lagos can't offer.
- **Language.** Most of the region works in English, and our written English is usually closer to what US and UK teams expect than recruiters assume.
- **Cost.** A senior Caribbean developer earning a strong US contractor rate is often still cheaper for the company than a US hire with benefits and payroll taxes. You don't need to undersell yourself to compete.
- **Overlap with the UK and Europe.** We're four or five hours behind London, so a UK team gets your mornings during their afternoons. That suits async teams well.

Put the overlap in plain numbers on your CV or profile: "Based in Trinidad (UTC-4, no DST). Available 9am to 5pm Eastern." Recruiters skim, and that one line answers their first worry.

## Where to find remote developer jobs that hire from the Caribbean

The hard part isn't finding remote jobs. It's finding ones that will hire someone outside the US, Canada or the EU. Many listings say "remote" and mean "remote within the United States", usually because of payroll, tax and work authorization rules.

| Source | What it's good for | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn Jobs | Volume and recruiter outreach | Filter by "Remote" and read the location line carefully |
| Remote-only job boards | Listings tagged "Worldwide" or by region | Many still mean US-only, so read the description |
| Stack-specific boards such as [LaraJobs](https://larajobs.com) | Laravel and PHP roles | Smaller volume, better fit |
| Startup job platforms | Early-stage companies open to contractors | Equity offers are often worthless to non-US residents |
| Agencies and dev shops in the US and UK | Contract work that turns into long-term roles | Rates vary widely |
| Your existing clients and network | Referrals, the highest hit rate by far | Takes time to build |

How to filter fast:

1. Search for "worldwide", "anywhere", "LATAM" or "Americas" alongside your stack. Latin America-focused hiring often includes the Caribbean, so say clearly that you're in the region.
2. Skip any listing that requires US work authorization or a US address unless it explicitly says contractors are welcome.
3. Look for companies that pay through an employer-of-record or contractor platform. If the listing mentions one, they already know how to pay someone outside their country.
4. Check the company's careers page for a list of countries they hire in. Many publish one.

## How to apply so you get past the first screen

Remote roles get hundreds of applications. Your CV is read by software first and a tired recruiter second.

**Make your CV parse cleanly.** One column, standard headings, no text in images or tables, and a PDF exported from a word processor. Use the exact technology names from the job description where they're true for you. I go through this properly in [how to write a developer resume that gets past ATS](/blog/developer-resume-ats).

**Tailor the top third.** A two-line summary that matches the role, followed by your three most relevant achievements. "Built and maintain a booking and payments system for an events business, with online card payments and a customer portal" says more than "Experienced full-stack developer passionate about clean code."

**Show real work.** Links to live sites and readable code beat certificates. If your client work is under NDA, build one small public project that shows how you structure code and write tests, and keep it tidy.

**Write a short, specific cover note.** Three short paragraphs: why this company, one example that matches what they need, and your time-zone overlap and availability. No life story.

**Apply the same week a role is posted.** Older listings are often already in final rounds.

## Skills remote employers look for in 2026

Remote teams hire for skills that let them trust you without seeing you.

- **Strong written communication.** Clear pull request descriptions, short status updates, asking good questions in writing. This is the skill most often missing.
- **Testing.** Remote teams rely on tests because nobody is looking over your shoulder. Show you write them.
- **Git and code review habits.** Small commits, clean branches, reviewing other people's code kindly.
- **Using AI tools responsibly.** Most teams expect you to use AI coding assistants now, and they want to know you review what they produce. If you're choosing one, see my comparison of [AI coding assistants](/blog/ai-coding-assistants).
- **One stack, done properly.** "Laravel and Vue, with tests and deployment" beats a list of fifteen frameworks.

## Getting paid in USD from the Caribbean

Sort this out before the offer, because "how do we pay you?" can stall a hire.

Most companies pay Caribbean developers in one of three ways:

1. **As a contractor through a platform.** Employer-of-record and contractor platforms let a company pay you without setting up a local entity. Withdrawal methods vary by country, so check what's available where you live before you rely on it.
2. **Direct invoice by wire transfer.** You invoice monthly in USD and the company sends a wire. Opening a US-dollar account at your local bank makes this simpler. Ask your bank about incoming wire fees and how long USD receipts take to clear.
3. **Payment services.** Payoneer is widely used by freelancers in Trinidad and Tobago and offers USD receiving details. PayPal works locally but costs more to receive and withdraw. Fees and features change, so compare current pricing before you choose.

A few practical points from experience:

- Agree the currency in writing. Being paid in USD and holding USD protects you from exchange-rate pain and from the foreign-exchange shortages Trinidad has dealt with for years.
- Ask who pays the transfer fees. Wire fees on both ends add up across a year.
- Keep every invoice and payment record in one place. You'll need them for tax.

## Taxes, contracts and the paperwork

This isn't tax advice, and you should speak to an accountant. But know the basics before you sign anything.

**Tax in Trinidad and Tobago.** Residents are generally taxed on their worldwide income, so pay from a foreign company is still taxable at home. [PwC's Trinidad and Tobago tax summary](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/trinidad-and-tobago/individual/income-determination) is a good overview. As a contractor you're self-employed, which means no PAYE: you're expected to pay estimated tax in quarterly instalments and file a return each year. Check the current forms and deadlines with the [Board of Inland Revenue](https://www.ird.gov.tt). Other Caribbean countries have their own rules, so check locally.

**Contracts.** Read for the following before signing:

- Who owns the code, and when ownership transfers (usually on payment)
- Notice period on both sides
- Payment terms, currency and late payment
- Which country's law applies
- Whether you can take other clients

**National Insurance, pension and health cover.** No employer is deducting or contributing anything for you. Ask your accountant or the National Insurance Board what applies to self-employed people, and plan your own pension and health insurance.

## Working remotely from the Caribbean without letting the region trip you up

The things that end remote contracts here are rarely skill. They're power, internet and communication during bad weather.

- **Have a backup connection.** Keep a mobile data plan that can run as a hotspot. Broadband outside the main towns can be patchy, and a dropped call during a client demo hurts.
- **Get a UPS for your router and laptop.** A short power cut shouldn't end your workday.
- **Plan for hurricane season.** It runs from June to November. Tell your team early if a storm is forecast, agree how you'll reach them, and keep your work pushed to the remote repository daily.
- **Keep business on business tools.** WhatsApp is how everyone here runs business, but most remote teams use Slack or Teams. Use their channels and keep your status updated.
- **Over-communicate at first.** A short end-of-day note for the first month (what you did, what's next, any blockers) builds trust faster than anything else.

## What to do next: a two-week plan

1. **Days 1 to 3:** Rewrite your CV for ATS, add your time-zone line, and choose the three projects that best show your work.
2. **Days 4 to 5:** Set up how you'll get paid: a USD account and one payment service, tested with a small transfer.
3. **Days 6 to 10:** Apply to five to ten well-matched roles a day that say "worldwide" or "Americas". Track them in a spreadsheet.
4. **Days 11 to 14:** Follow up on anything older than a week, message people you've worked with to ask about openings, and polish one public project.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can Caribbean developers work for US companies remotely?

Yes, usually as independent contractors rather than employees. US companies generally can't put you on their payroll without a local entity, so they pay you on invoice or through an employer-of-record or contractor platform. Look for listings marked "worldwide" or "Americas", and avoid ones that require US work authorization unless they say contractors are welcome.

### How much do remote developers in the Caribbean earn?

It varies a lot with seniority, stack and how you're hired. Caribbean developers working for US or UK companies typically earn well above local salaries, but usually below what a US-based employee would make in the same role. Research contractor rates for your stack and experience level on job boards that publish salaries, and quote in US dollars.

### Do I need a degree to get a remote developer job?

Many remote employers don't require one, especially startups and agencies. What they want is evidence that you can do the work: live projects, readable code, tests and clear communication. A degree helps with some larger companies and with immigration if you ever relocate. Shipped work and references matter more for most remote roles.

### How do I get paid from overseas in Trinidad and Tobago?

The common options are a wire transfer into a US-dollar account at a local bank, Payoneer, PayPal, or a contractor platform the employer uses. Fees and availability change, so compare current costs and test with a small payment first. Keep records of every payment because residents are generally taxed on worldwide income.

### What time zone is Trinidad and Tobago in for remote work?

Trinidad and Tobago uses Atlantic Standard Time, UTC-4, with no daylight saving. That puts it on the same time as New York from March to November and one hour ahead from November to March. It's four or five hours behind London, depending on the time of year.
